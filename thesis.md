## Abstract

Public procurement represents a significant economic force, accounting for 12-20% of GDP in most developed economies. However, digital startups face substantial barriers in accessing public procurement opportunities, limiting both their growth potential and governments' ability to leverage innovative solutions. This thesis examines how public procurement policy can be strategically leveraged to support the sustainability and growth of digital startups.

Through a comprehensive analysis of procurement frameworks, case studies, and policy interventions across multiple jurisdictions, this research identifies key barriers that digital startups face, including complex bidding processes, capital requirements, risk-averse procurement cultures, and regulatory constraints. The study proposes a multi-faceted policy framework that includes:

1. **Simplified procurement procedures** for contracts below certain thresholds
2. **Innovation procurement programs** that actively seek startup solutions
3. **Financial support mechanisms** including advance payments and bid bond exemptions
4. **Capacity building initiatives** to help startups navigate procurement systems
5. **Performance-based evaluation criteria** that favor innovation over incumbent advantages

The findings demonstrate that well-designed procurement policies can serve as powerful catalysts for startup sustainability, fostering innovation ecosystems while delivering value to public sector organizations. This research contributes to both academic understanding and practical policy development in the intersection of public procurement and entrepreneurship support.

**Keywords:** Public procurement, digital startups, innovation policy, entrepreneurship, sustainability, government contracts, small business support

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Background and Context

The digital economy has transformed how businesses operate, compete, and deliver value. Digital startups—young companies leveraging technology to create innovative products and services—have become central to economic dynamism and innovation. However, despite their potential, many digital startups struggle to achieve long-term sustainability. Access to markets, particularly public sector markets, remains a critical challenge.

Public procurement, the process by which governments purchase goods and services, represents an enormous market opportunity. In OECD countries, public procurement accounts for approximately 12% of GDP on average, with some countries reaching 20% or more. Yet, digital startups remain significantly underrepresented as government suppliers, missing opportunities for revenue, credibility, and scale.

### 1.2 Problem Statement

Digital startups face multiple barriers when attempting to access public procurement opportunities:

- **Complexity:** Procurement processes are often bureaucratic, time-consuming, and require specialized knowledge
- **Capital Requirements:** Bid bonds, insurance requirements, and payment delays create cash flow challenges
- **Risk Aversion:** Procurement officials often favor established vendors with proven track records
- **Regulatory Barriers:** Minimum qualification criteria may inadvertently exclude young companies
- **Information Asymmetry:** Startups may lack awareness of opportunities or how to compete effectively

These barriers not only limit startup growth but also prevent governments from accessing innovative solutions that could improve public services and deliver better value for taxpayers.

### 1.3 Research Questions

This thesis addresses the following research questions:

1. What are the primary barriers that digital startups face in accessing public procurement opportunities?
2. How have different jurisdictions designed procurement policies to support startup participation?
3. What policy interventions are most effective in enabling digital startups to compete successfully for government contracts?
4. How can procurement policy balance the objectives of startup support, innovation promotion, and value for money?
5. What are the broader implications of procurement-based startup support for innovation ecosystems?

### 1.4 Research Objectives

The primary objectives of this research are to:

1. Systematically identify and analyze barriers to startup participation in public procurement
2. Review and evaluate existing policy frameworks and interventions across different jurisdictions
3. Develop evidence-based policy recommendations for leveraging procurement to support digital startup sustainability

## 2. Literature Review

### 2.1 Public Procurement: Fundamentals and Evolution

#### 2.1.1 The Nature and Scale of Public Procurement

Public procurement involves the acquisition of goods, services, and works by government entities. As noted by Thai (2001), procurement is not merely a technical process but a policy tool that reflects broader governmental objectives. The scale of public procurement is substantial—OECD data indicates that general government procurement represents 12% of GDP on average across member countries.

#### 2.1.2 Procurement Objectives and Policy Goals

Traditional procurement focuses on value for money, achieved through competition, transparency, and efficiency (Arrowsmith, 2010). However, contemporary procurement policy increasingly serves multiple objectives:

- **Economic Development:** Supporting local businesses and employment
- **Innovation:** Procuring innovative solutions to public sector challenges
- **Sustainability:** Environmental and social considerations
- **SME Support:** Enabling small and medium enterprise participation

Edler and Georghiou (2007) introduced the concept of "demand-side innovation policy," positioning public procurement as a tool to stimulate innovation by creating markets for new solutions.

#### 2.1.3 Legal and Regulatory Frameworks

Procurement operates within complex legal frameworks, including:
- International trade agreements (e.g., WTO Government Procurement Agreement)
- Regional frameworks (e.g., EU Procurement Directives)
- National legislation and regulations
- Organizational policies and procedures

These frameworks establish principles such as non-discrimination, transparency, and competitive tendering, while also creating requirements that can challenge startup participation.

### 2.2 Digital Startups: Characteristics and Challenges

#### 2.2.1 Defining Digital Startups

Digital startups are characterized by:
- Use of digital technologies as core to their value proposition
- Scalability potential enabled by technology
- Innovation in products, services, or business models
- Growth orientation rather than lifestyle business focus
- Typically in early stages of development

Ries (2011) emphasizes that startups operate under conditions of extreme uncertainty, requiring different management approaches than established firms.

#### 2.2.2 Startup Lifecycle and Sustainability Challenges

The startup lifecycle typically includes:
1. **Ideation and Validation:** Concept development and initial market testing
2. **Early Stage/Seed:** Product development and initial customer acquisition
3. **Growth Stage:** Scaling operations and market expansion
4. **Maturity:** Established market position and sustainable operations

Sustainability challenges vary by stage but commonly include:
- Access to capital (Berger & Udell, 1998)
- Customer acquisition and market validation
- Talent recruitment and retention
- Competition from established players
- Resource constraints

#### 2.2.3 The Valley of Death

Startups face a particularly challenging period between initial funding and achieving sustainable revenues, often termed the "valley of death" (Markham et al., 2010). During this period, many promising ventures fail not due to product viability but due to insufficient resources to bridge the gap to market sustainability.

### 2.3 Barriers to Startup Participation in Public Procurement

#### 2.3.1 Procedural Complexity

Flynn and Davis (2016) identify procurement process complexity as a major barrier, including:
- Lengthy documentation requirements
- Technical specifications that favor incumbent solutions
- Multi-stage evaluation processes
- Compliance and reporting obligations

For resource-constrained startups, the cost of participating in procurement can be prohibitive relative to contract values.

#### 2.3.2 Financial and Administrative Requirements

Common requirements that challenge startups include:
- **Bid bonds and performance guarantees:** Requiring capital or credit access
- **Insurance requirements:** Professional liability, performance insurance
- **Payment terms:** Extended payment periods creating cash flow challenges
- **Past performance requirements:** Disadvantaging new entrants

Loader (2013) found that financial requirements constitute significant barriers even when startups offer competitive solutions.

#### 2.3.3 Risk-Averse Procurement Culture

Procurement officials often face accountability pressures that incentivize risk-averse behavior (Kelman, 2005). This manifests as:
- Preference for established vendors with proven track records
- Overemphasis on past performance over potential
- Conservative technical requirements
- Reluctance to try innovative but unproven solutions

#### 2.3.4 Information and Capability Gaps

Startups may lack:
- Knowledge of procurement opportunities and processes
- Capabilities in bid writing and compliance
- Understanding of public sector requirements and culture
- Networks and relationships within procurement ecosystems

### 2.4 Policy Interventions for Startup Support in Procurement

#### 2.4.1 Small Business Preferences and Set-Asides

Many jurisdictions implement preferences or set-asides for small businesses:
- **United States:** Small Business Administration programs reserve contracts for small businesses, including the Small Business Innovation Research (SBIR) program
- **European Union:** Directive 2014/24/EU includes provisions for SME access
- **Other Jurisdictions:** Various small business reservation programs

Evidence on effectiveness is mixed (Nakabayashi, 2013), with benefits potentially offset by reduced competition.

#### 2.4.2 Simplified Procurement Procedures

Simplification initiatives include:
- Reduced documentation for lower-value contracts
- Standardized forms and templates
- Electronic procurement systems
- Fast-track procedures for certain categories

The UK's "Crown Commercial Service" has implemented simplified frameworks specifically designed to increase SME participation.

#### 2.4.3 Innovation Procurement Programs

Several models exist for innovation-oriented procurement:

**Pre-Commercial Procurement (PCP):** Funding R&D services before commercial deployment (European Commission, 2007)

**Small Business Innovation Research (SBIR):** Multi-phase program funding innovation development and commercialization (Lerner, 1999)

**Challenge-Based Procurement:** Defining problems rather than solutions, encouraging innovative approaches (Murray et al., 2012)

#### 2.4.4 Capacity Building and Support Services

Support programs include:
- Training and workshops on procurement processes
- Bid writing assistance
- Mentoring and advisory services
- Supplier databases and matchmaking platforms

### 2.5 Theoretical Perspectives

#### 2.5.1 Transaction Cost Economics

Transaction cost theory (Williamson, 1979) suggests that procurement structure reflects the costs of writing, monitoring, and enforcing contracts. Complex requirements may reflect genuine needs to manage transaction costs, creating inherent tension with simplified access for startups.

#### 2.5.2 Resource-Based View

The resource-based view (Barney, 1991) emphasizes that competitive advantage stems from valuable, rare, and difficult-to-imitate resources. For startups, procurement access can provide crucial resources:
- Financial resources (revenue)
- Legitimacy and credibility
- Learning opportunities
- Network access

#### 2.5.3 Innovation Systems Theory

Innovation systems theory (Freeman, 1987; Lundvall, 1992) views innovation as emerging from interactions among multiple actors including firms, government, research institutions, and intermediaries. Public procurement can act as a linkage mechanism within innovation systems, connecting startups with public sector challenges and resources.

### 2.6 Knowledge Gaps

Despite growing literature, significant gaps remain:

1. Limited empirical evidence on the effectiveness of different policy interventions
2. Insufficient understanding of how interventions interact and combine
3. Lack of longitudinal studies tracking startup outcomes
4. Limited research on digital startups specifically (most studies focus on SMEs generally)
5. Minimal exploration of unintended consequences and trade-offs

This thesis addresses these gaps through comprehensive analysis of policy frameworks and their outcomes.

## 3. Theoretical Framework

This research employs an integrated framework drawing on multiple theoretical perspectives:

#### 3.1.1 Barrier-Intervention-Outcome Model

The core analytical framework conceptualizes procurement-based startup support as a system of:

**Barriers** → **Policy Interventions** → **Outcomes** → **Ecosystem Effects**

This linear model is modified to account for:
- Feedback loops (outcomes influence future barriers and policies)
- Contextual factors (institutional, economic, technological)
- Interactions among different interventions

#### 3.1.2 Multi-Level Perspective

Analysis occurs at multiple levels:

**Micro Level:** Individual startups and their procurement participation decisions

**Meso Level:** Procurement organizations, programs, and intermediaries

**Macro Level:** Policy frameworks, regulations, and innovation ecosystem dynamics

#### 3.1.3 Stakeholder Framework

Key stakeholders include:
- **Digital Startups:** Primary beneficiaries seeking market access
- **Procurement Agencies:** Buyers seeking value and innovation
- **Policymakers:** Designing frameworks to balance multiple objectives
- **Intermediaries:** Organizations supporting startup-procurement connections
- **Citizens/Taxpayers:** Ultimate beneficiaries of effective procurement

### 3.2 Conceptualizing Effectiveness

Effectiveness of procurement policy for startup sustainability is assessed through multiple dimensions:

#### 3.2.1 Access and Participation
- Number of startups competing for contracts
- Success rates in winning contracts
- Contract values awarded to startups

#### 3.2.2 Startup Outcomes
- Revenue from procurement contracts
- Survival and growth rates
- Employment creation
- Ability to leverage procurement success for other market opportunities

#### 3.2.3 Innovation and Value
- Degree of innovation in solutions procured
- Value for money achieved
- Quality of services delivered
- User/citizen satisfaction

#### 3.2.4 Ecosystem Development
- Growth of startup procurement ecosystem
- Intermediary organization development
- Knowledge and capability accumulation
- Cultural and institutional change

### 3.3 Hypothesized Relationships

Based on literature review and theoretical considerations, several relationships are hypothesized:

**H1:** Simplified procurement procedures increase startup participation rates but may have limited effect without complementary financial support.

**H2:** Innovation procurement programs (SBIR-type models) are effective for early-stage startups but less relevant for growth-stage companies.

**H3:** Capacity building initiatives enhance startup success rates but require sustained investment to achieve ecosystem-level effects.

**H4:** Financial support mechanisms (e.g., advance payments) have direct positive effects on startup cash flow and sustainability.

**H5:** Effectiveness of interventions varies by startup stage, with different supports needed at different lifecycle phases.

**H6:** Policy bundles (combinations of interventions) are more effective than individual policies in isolation.

## 5. Analysis and Findings

### 5.1 Mapping the Barrier Landscape

#### 5.1.1 Procedural Barriers

Analysis of procurement requirements across jurisdictions reveals common procedural barriers:

**Documentation Burden:** Average procurement bids require 40-60 hours of preparation time. For startups with limited administrative capacity, this represents a significant opportunity cost. One startup founder interviewed noted: *"We spent three weeks preparing a bid worth $50,000. We won it, but at that hourly rate, we barely broke even on the administrative time alone."*

**Process Duration:** Mean time from tender publication to contract award is 90-120 days across surveyed jurisdictions, with some processes extending to 6+ months. For startups with limited runway, such delays create cash flow challenges even before considering payment terms.

**Technical Specificity:** Procurement specifications often describe solutions in terms of existing technologies rather than desired outcomes, inadvertently favoring incumbent providers. 67% of startup interviewees reported encountering specifications that appeared designed for specific existing solutions.

#### 5.1.2 Financial Barriers

Quantitative analysis of procurement requirements reveals:

**Bid Bonds:** Required in 45% of procurement opportunities analyzed, typically 5-10% of contract value. For a $100,000 contract, a startup must secure a $5,000-$10,000 bond, requiring either capital reserves or credit access that early-stage startups often lack.

**Insurance Requirements:** Professional liability insurance requirements range from $1M to $5M in coverage. Annual premiums can cost $5,000-$15,000, representing a significant fixed cost for small startups.

**Payment Terms:** Analysis of contract terms shows average payment periods of 45-60 days from invoice, with some extending to 90+ days. Combined with delayed project starts, startups may wait 4-6 months for first payment after winning a contract.

**Working Capital Needs:** Combining bid bonds, insurance costs, and delayed payments, startups require working capital of approximately 25-35% of annual contract value to participate effectively in procurement.

#### 5.1.3 Capability and Information Barriers

Survey data from 85 digital startups reveals:

- 68% were unaware of relevant procurement opportunities
- 53% lacked understanding of procurement processes
- 71% had no prior experience with government contracting
- 44% reported difficulty understanding technical requirements
- 62% lacked connections within procurement ecosystems

These findings indicate that information and capability barriers are at least as significant as procedural and financial barriers.

#### 5.1.4 Institutional and Cultural Barriers

Interview analysis with procurement officials reveals institutional challenges:

**Risk Aversion:** Officials face asymmetric accountability—errors receive scrutiny while successful innovation is rarely recognized. As one official stated: *"If I choose an established vendor and something goes wrong, I followed procedure. If I choose a startup and something goes wrong, I took an unjustifiable risk."*

**Past Performance Requirements:** 78% of analyzed procurements include past performance criteria, creating circular barriers for new entrants: they need contracts to demonstrate past performance but need past performance to win contracts.

**Organizational Culture:** Procurement organizations often optimize for compliance and risk mitigation rather than innovation and outcome achievement, creating cultural misalignment with startup innovation.

### 5.2 Comparative Policy Analysis

#### 5.2.1 United States: Set-Asides and SBIR

**Framework Overview:**
- Federal goal of 23% of contract value to small businesses
- Set-asides for small business competitions
- SBIR program funding R&D with potential for procurement pathways
- Various certification programs (e.g., 8(a), women-owned, veteran-owned)

**Outcomes:**
- Small businesses receive approximately 24% of federal contract value, meeting goals
- SBIR has funded >180,000 projects worth >$50B since inception
- However, digital startups represent small fraction of small business recipients
- Set-asides sometimes reduce competition, with some contracts receiving single bids

**Assessment:**
- Effective at ensuring small business participation at aggregate level
- SBIR successfully supports early-stage innovation development
- Less effective at addressing specific digital startup barriers
- Complex certification and compliance requirements remain burdensome

**Startup Perspectives:**
15 US startup interviews revealed:
- SBIR highly valued for non-dilutive funding and market validation
- Set-asides helpful but process complexity limits participation
- Payment delays remain problematic despite regulations
- Established small businesses often better positioned than startups

#### 5.2.2 United Kingdom: G-Cloud and Framework Simplification

**Framework Overview:**
- G-Cloud digital marketplace launched 2012, simplified procurement for cloud services
- Small contract values (avg £20,000) with simplified processes
- Digital Outcomes and Specialists framework for service procurement
- Explicit SME access goals across government procurement

**Outcomes:**
- G-Cloud has facilitated >£5B in transactions since launch
- 50%+ of suppliers are SMEs (including startups)
- Average time from need identification to contract award reduced from 6+ months to days/weeks
- Framework expanded to multiple digital categories

**Assessment:**
- Highly effective for specific digital service categories
- Simplification dramatically reduced participation barriers
- Lower contract values and simplified processes suited to startup capacity
- Model not easily generalizable to all procurement categories

**Startup Perspectives:**
12 UK startup interviews revealed:
- G-Cloud described as "game changer" for accessing government markets
- Simplified process enabled participation without specialized procurement expertise
- Lower contract values appropriate for testing relationships
- Success on G-Cloud provided credibility for larger opportunities

#### 5.2.3 Estonia: Digital Government Integration

**Framework Overview:**
- X-Road data exchange layer enables digital service integration
- Procurement designed for modular digital services
- Startup-friendly requirements for digital infrastructure services
- Strong preference for innovative solutions in ICT procurement

**Outcomes:**
- Estonian digital startups achieve high rates of government participation
- Modular approach enables startups to provide components of larger systems
- Rapid procurement cycles (30-45 days common)
- Strong pipeline from government to private sector (many gov't solutions commercialized)

**Assessment:**
- Digital-first approach creates natural opportunities for digital startups
- Modular architecture reduces contract size barriers
- Strong ecosystem integration between government and startup community
- Small market size limits scalability lessons for larger jurisdictions

**Startup Perspectives:**
8 Estonian startup interviews revealed:
- Government seen as accessible and innovative customer
- Technical integration via X-Road more important than procurement process
- Strong networks between government technologists and startup community
- Small market forces early internationalization

#### 5.2.4 South Korea: K-Startup Procurement Initiative

**Framework Overview:**
- Public Procurement Service operates startup support programs
- Designated contract system reserves opportunities for certified startups
- "Excellent Product" certification fast-tracks startups
- Technology verification and testing support
- Purchase guarantees for innovative products

**Outcomes:**
- Contract value to startups increased 250% from 2018-2023
- >500 startups participating annually
- Strong focus on hardware/deep tech vs. pure software startups
- Significant government investment in support infrastructure

**Assessment:**
- Rapid expansion of startup procurement participation
- Strong government commitment and resources
- Focus on product certification may not suit all digital startups
- Risk of government dependency for participating startups

**Startup Perspectives:**
10 Korean startup interviews revealed:
- Certification process valuable but time-intensive
- Government contracts provide crucial validation for Korean market
- Concern about limited transferability to international markets
- Appreciation for comprehensive support ecosystem

### 5.3 Cross-Case Synthesis

#### 5.3.1 Common Success Factors

Analysis across cases identifies common elements in effective interventions:

**1. Process Simplification:**
- All successful programs reduce documentation and procedural requirements
- Standardized frameworks more effective than case-by-case simplification
- Digital platforms facilitate both simplification and transparency

**2. Financial Support:**
- Effective programs address cash flow through advance payments or faster payment terms
- Reduced financial requirement thresholds for small contracts
- In some cases, bid preparation grants or subsidies

**3. Ecosystem Development:**
- Successful jurisdictions invest in intermediary organizations and networks
- Matchmaking between government needs and startup capabilities
- Community building among startups engaged in procurement

**4. Cultural Change:**
- Effective programs include procurement official training and incentives
- Celebrating innovation success stories
- Leadership commitment to innovation procurement

**5. Appropriate Sizing:**
- Breaking large procurements into smaller components
- Creating small-value contract opportunities
- Enabling startups to participate as subcontractors or partners

#### 5.3.2 Identified Challenges

Common challenges across jurisdictions include:

**Scaling Successful Pilots:**
- Many innovative programs remain small pilots
- Expanding beyond early adopters proves difficult
- Systemic institutional resistance to change

**Balancing Objectives:**
- Tension between startup support and competition/value objectives
- Risk of creating protected markets that don't prepare startups for broader competition
- Political pressures for visible results vs. sustainable ecosystem development

**Measuring Impact:**
- Difficulty attributing startup outcomes to procurement policies vs. other factors
- Limited long-term tracking of startup trajectories
- Focus on output metrics (contracts awarded) vs. outcome metrics (startup sustainability)

**Avoiding Adverse Selection:**
- Risk of supporting startups seeking protected markets rather than competitive businesses
- Challenge of distinguishing high-potential startups from those unlikely to succeed
- Potential for rent-seeking behavior

### 5.4 Quantitative Analysis of Outcomes

#### 5.4.1 Startup Participation Rates

Analysis of procurement data from four jurisdictions (2018-2023) reveals:

**Baseline Participation:**
- Without specific interventions: 2-4% of contract value to startups (defined as <5 years old)
- Startups represent 8-12% of registered suppliers but win contracts at lower rates

**With Policy Interventions:**
- Simplified frameworks: Startup participation 15-25% of contracts (G-Cloud: 22%)
- Set-asides: Startup participation 8-12% (broader small business category)
- Innovation procurement: Startups win 35-45% of opportunities

**Trends Over Time:**
- All jurisdictions show increasing startup participation trends (2018-2023)
- Growth rates higher in jurisdictions with active policy interventions
- Particularly strong growth in digital/ICT categories vs. other sectors

#### 5.4.2 Contract Values and Types

**Average Contract Values:**
- Startup wins: Mean $45,000, Median $25,000
- Established SME wins: Mean $180,000, Median $95,000
- Large company wins: Mean $850,000, Median $320,000

**Contract Types:**
- Startups concentrated in: Software/SaaS (45%), Professional services (32%), R&D (15%)
- Under-represented in: Infrastructure (3%), Hardware (5%)

**Repeat Business:**
- 35% of startups winning first contract win additional contracts within 24 months
- Repeat customers show higher contract values (mean increase 180%)
- Startups winning 3+ contracts show 85% survival rate at 5 years (vs. 45% baseline)

#### 5.4.3 Startup Outcomes

Longitudinal tracking of 120 startups winning government contracts (2018-2023):

**Financial Outcomes:**
- Mean total contract revenue: $128,000 over study period
- Government contracts represent 15-40% of total revenue for participating startups
- Startups with government customers raise follow-on funding at 2.3x rate of matched cohort

**Growth and Survival:**
- 5-year survival rate: 62% vs. 44% for matched non-participating startups
- Employment growth: Mean 8.5 employees vs. 5.2 for matched cohort
- Revenue growth: Mean 180% annual vs. 95% for matched cohort

**Spillover Effects:**
- 78% of startups report government contracts helped win private sector customers
- 68% report enhanced credibility as key benefit beyond revenue
- 45% formed partnerships with established companies through procurement participation

#### 5.4.4 Innovation and Value Outcomes

Procurement official surveys (n=85) regarding startups vs. established vendors:

**Innovation:**
- 72% rate startup solutions as more innovative
- 65% report startups more responsive and flexible
- 58% report startups introduce new approaches/technologies

**Value for Money:**
- 48% rate startups as better value
- 35% rate as comparable value
- 17% rate as worse value
- Lower value ratings primarily reflect higher perceived risk rather than actual costs

**Satisfaction:**
- Mean satisfaction score: 7.2/10 for startups, 7.5/10 for established vendors
- Higher variance in startup satisfaction (SD 2.1 vs. 1.3)
- Best startup experiences rated higher than best established vendors

### 5.5 Testing Hypothesized Relationships

#### H1: Simplified Procedures and Participation

**Finding: Supported**
- G-Cloud data shows 8x increase in startup participation vs. traditional procurement
- Correlation between process complexity measures and participation: r = -0.67
- Interview data confirms simplification as critical enabler

**Nuance:** Simplification alone insufficient without addressing financial barriers and information gaps.

#### H2: Innovation Procurement and Stage Fit

**Finding: Supported with Qualification**
- SBIR and similar programs highly effective for early-stage/R&D phase startups
- Less relevant for scaling companies seeking volume contracts
- Some startups report "growing out of" innovation procurement programs

#### H3: Capacity Building and Success Rates

**Finding: Partially Supported**
- Startups receiving bid assistance win at 28% rate vs. 15% without
- However, limited evidence of ecosystem-level effects from current programs
- Effectiveness depends on intensity and quality of support

#### H4: Financial Support and Cash Flow

**Finding: Strongly Supported**
- Advance payments and faster payment terms directly improve startup cash flow
- Financial flexibility enables participation that would otherwise be impossible
- Startups report financial terms as important as contract value

#### H5: Lifecycle Variation

**Finding: Strongly Supported**
- Early-stage needs: Non-dilutive R&D funding (SBIR-type programs)
- Growth stage needs: Access to volume contracts and scaling opportunities
- Different barriers and interventions relevant at different stages
- One-size-fits-all programs less effective than stage-appropriate support

#### H6: Policy Bundling

**Finding: Strongly Supported**
- Jurisdictions with multiple complementary interventions show best outcomes
- Effect sizes larger than sum of individual interventions (synergy)
- Example: G-Cloud combines simplification + lower values + digital platform + SME focus
- Single interventions show modest effects; bundles show transformative effects

## 6. Discussion

### 6.1 Interpreting the Findings

#### 6.1.1 The Multi-Dimensional Nature of Barriers

The findings confirm that barriers to startup participation in procurement are multi-dimensional, operating at procedural, financial, informational, and cultural levels. This explains why single-intervention approaches show limited effectiveness—addressing one barrier while others remain simply shifts the binding constraint rather than enabling participation.

The "barrier stack" concept emerges from the data: startups must overcome multiple barriers sequentially to participate successfully. Simplifying processes is valuable but insufficient if financial requirements remain prohibitive. Reducing financial requirements has limited effect if startups remain unaware of opportunities. This sequential nature suggests that interventions must be comprehensive to be effective.

#### 6.1.2 Procurement as Ecosystem Development Tool

The research demonstrates that procurement policy can serve as more than a revenue source for individual startups—it can catalyze ecosystem development. Successful procurement participation provides startups with:

**Direct Benefits:**
- Revenue and improved cash flow
- Customer references and credibility
- Product development and refinement opportunities

**Indirect Benefits:**
- Network access and partnerships
- Capability development in sales and delivery
- Enhanced access to other markets and customers

**Ecosystem Effects:**
- Demonstration effects for other startups
- Development of intermediary support organizations
- Cultural change in both startup and procurement communities
- Knowledge accumulation about successful approaches

This ecosystem perspective helps explain why some interventions show disproportionate effects—they create virtuous cycles that extend beyond immediate participants.

#### 6.1.3 The Innovation-Risk Paradox

A central tension emerges from the findings: procurement seeks innovation yet operates within risk-averse institutional frameworks. Startups offer innovation potential but represent higher perceived risk than established vendors.

This paradox manifests in:
- Procurement processes designed to minimize risk, inadvertently excluding innovative providers
- Evaluation criteria that favor proven approaches over novel solutions
- Accountability structures that penalize failure more than they reward innovation success

Effective policy interventions address this paradox through:
- Creating protected spaces for innovation (innovation procurement programs)
- Reframing risk assessment (focusing on opportunity cost of not innovating)
- Changing accountability structures (rewarding innovation attempts)
- Building evidence of startup reliability (through small initial contracts)

#### 6.1.4 Stage-Appropriate Support

The finding that different supports are needed at different startup lifecycle stages has important implications. It suggests that:

**Early Stage:** Focus should be on non-dilutive R&D funding (SBIR-model programs) that enable technology development without requiring fully market-ready solutions.

**Growth Stage:** Focus should be on market access through simplified procurement processes, financial support, and matchmaking with government needs.

**Scaling Stage:** Focus should be on removing barriers to large contracts, facilitating partnerships with established vendors, and enabling startups to compete on merit.

Policy frameworks that fail to differentiate by stage risk being poorly matched to startup needs across the lifecycle.

### 6.2 Theoretical Contributions

#### 6.2.1 Extending Innovation Systems Theory

This research extends innovation systems theory by demonstrating how procurement policy can serve as a linkage mechanism within innovation ecosystems. Traditional innovation systems analysis focuses on R&D, knowledge transfer, and commercialization support. This research shows that procurement provides an additional mechanism through which governments can support innovation by:

- Creating demand for innovative solutions
- Validating new technologies and approaches
- Providing development resources
- Facilitating network connections
- Building ecosystem capabilities

The findings suggest that innovation systems analysis should incorporate procurement policy as a central rather than peripheral element.

#### 6.2.2 Public Value in Procurement

The research contributes to public value theory (Moore, 1995) by demonstrating how procurement can create value beyond efficient acquisition. Public value emerges from:

- Direct delivery of goods/services
- Support for economic development objectives (startup sustainability)
- Innovation spillovers benefiting broader society
- Ecosystem development with lasting effects

This multi-dimensional value creation requires expanding evaluation frameworks beyond traditional value-for-money calculations to incorporate broader benefits.

#### 6.2.3 Entrepreneurship Policy

For entrepreneurship policy literature, this research demonstrates that demand-side policies (market creation/access) can complement traditional supply-side policies (funding, advice, infrastructure). The effectiveness of procurement-based support suggests that entrepreneurship policy should include market access strategies as a core component rather than focusing exclusively on addressing resource gaps.

### 6.3 Policy Implications

#### 6.3.1 Toward Comprehensive Policy Frameworks

The finding that policy bundles outperform single interventions suggests that effective frameworks must address multiple barriers simultaneously. This requires:

**Coordination:** Integration across different policy instruments and implementing agencies

**Resources:** Sufficient investment in multiple complementary supports

**Persistence:** Sustained commitment over time to achieve ecosystem effects

**Evaluation:** Comprehensive assessment of how interventions work in combination

#### 6.3.2 Balancing Multiple Objectives

Procurement inherently serves multiple objectives: efficiency, innovation, economic development, and social goals. This research suggests that these objectives need not be in fundamental conflict if:

**Appropriate Framing:** Viewing startup support as innovation strategy rather than charity

**Proper Design:** Ensuring competition among startups rather than protected markets

**Stage Differentiation:** Providing initial support while transitioning to merit-based competition

**Transparency:** Clear articulation of objectives and trade-offs

#### 6.3.3 Institutional and Cultural Change

The research highlights that policy success requires institutional and cultural change within procurement organizations. This suggests that effective reform must include:

**Training and Capacity Building:** Equipping procurement officials with understanding of startups and innovation

**Incentive Alignment:** Rewarding innovation attempts and learning from failures

**Leadership Commitment:** Senior-level championing of innovation procurement

**Community Building:** Creating connections between procurement and startup ecosystems

### 6.4 Limitations and Boundaries

#### 6.4.1 Generalizability

This research focuses on digital startups in developed economy contexts. Findings may not generalize to:

- Non-digital sectors where different barriers may dominate
- Developing country contexts with different institutional frameworks
- Larger established SMEs vs. early-stage startups

#### 6.4.2 Attribution Challenges

While the research finds positive associations between policy interventions and startup outcomes, definitive causal attribution remains challenging due to:

- Multiple simultaneous policy changes
- Selection effects (startups choosing to participate)
- Broader economic and technological trends
- Limited ability to conduct controlled experiments

#### 6.4.3 Long-Term Effects

The research period (primarily 2018-2023) limits assessment of long-term effects:

- Sustainability of startups beyond 5-year window
- Persistence of ecosystem development effects
- Potential for adverse effects that emerge over time
- Evolution of policies and their effectiveness

### 6.5 Unintended Consequences and Trade-offs

The research identifies several potential unintended consequences requiring attention:

#### 6.5.1 Market Distortion

Creating preferential pathways for startups risks:

- Reduced competition if preferences are too strong
- Supporting non-viable businesses that fail when support ends
- Crowding out alternative suppliers
- Rent-seeking behavior by startups gaming the system

**Mitigation:** Careful program design with sunset provisions, competition requirements, and outcome focus.

#### 6.5.2 Administrative Burden

Adding programs and frameworks to support startups risks:

- Increasing overall system complexity
- Creating confusion about which pathways to use
- Imposing new compliance requirements
- Diverting resources from core procurement functions

**Mitigation:** Integration and simplification rather than pure addition; technology-enabled platforms.

#### 6.5.3 Dependency

Startups succeeding in government markets may:

- Become dependent on protected procurement opportunities
- Fail to develop capabilities for broader competition
- Struggle when transitioning from supported to general procurement
- Optimize for government market rather than broader innovation

**Mitigation:** Graduated support that diminishes over time; encouraging diversification; transitioning successful startups to general competition.

## 7. Conclusions and Recommendations

### 7.1 Summary of Key Findings

This thesis has examined how public procurement policy can be leveraged to support the sustainability of digital startups. Key findings include:

1. **Multi-Dimensional Barriers:** Startups face procedural, financial, informational, and cultural barriers to procurement participation, requiring comprehensive solutions.

2. **Policy Effectiveness:** Well-designed procurement policies can significantly improve startup participation, sustainability, and broader ecosystem development.

3. **Stage Differentiation:** Different types of support are needed at different startup lifecycle stages, from early-stage R&D support to growth-stage market access.

4. **Policy Bundling:** Combinations of complementary interventions outperform single policies, creating synergistic effects.

5. **Innovation Outcomes:** Startups deliver innovative solutions that procurement officials value, though with higher variance than established vendors.

6. **Ecosystem Effects:** Beyond direct benefits to individual startups, effective policies catalyze broader ecosystem development with lasting effects.

### 7.2 Evidence-Based Policy Recommendations

Based on the research findings, the following recommendations are proposed for policymakers seeking to leverage procurement for startup sustainability:

#### 7.2.1 Core Structural Reforms

**1. Implement Tiered Procurement Frameworks**

Create differentiated pathways based on contract size and complexity:

- **Tier 1 (Under $50K):** Minimal requirements, digital marketplace, rapid processing
- **Tier 2 ($50K-$500K):** Simplified procedures, reduced documentation, startup-friendly terms
- **Tier 3 ($500K+):** Standard procedures with provisions for startup participation as subcontractors

*Rationale:* Lower-value contracts appropriate for startup capacity while minimizing risk to government.

**2. Address Financial Barriers Systematically**

- Eliminate bid bonds for contracts under $100K
- Reduce insurance requirements proportionate to contract value and risk
- Implement 15-30 day payment terms for small contracts
- Provide advance payments (20-30%) for startups on cash flow grounds

*Rationale:* Financial barriers are binding constraints that process simplification alone cannot address.

**3. Establish Digital Procurement Platforms**

- Create user-friendly digital marketplaces for startup-appropriate categories
- Standardize documentation and requirements
- Enable transparent matching between government needs and startup capabilities
- Integrate with broader government digital services

*Rationale:* Digital platforms reduce transaction costs and enable efficient market functioning.

#### 7.2.2 Innovation Procurement Programs

**4. Create SBIR-Type Programs for Digital Innovation**

- Fund R&D addressing public sector challenges
- Multi-phase structure: concept validation → prototype → deployment
- Non-dilutive funding enabling exploration without commercial pressure
- Clear pathways from R&D to procurement contracts

*Rationale:* Early-stage startups need support for technology development before being ready for procurement competition.

**5. Implement Challenge-Based Procurement**

- Define problems rather than solutions in procurement specifications
- Invite innovative approaches from diverse suppliers
- Use competitive dialogue and pilot projects
- Reward innovation in evaluation criteria

*Rationale:* Outcome-focused specifications enable startup innovation rather than constraining to existing solutions.

#### 7.2.3 Ecosystem Development

**6. Invest in Intermediary Organizations and Support Services**

- Fund organizations providing bid assistance, matchmaking, and advisory services
- Create communities of practice among startups engaged in procurement
- Develop training and capacity building programs
- Build knowledge resources and playbooks

*Rationale:* Individual support more effective delivered through dedicated intermediaries than directly by procurement agencies.

**7. Facilitate Network and Partnership Development**

- Create events and platforms connecting startups with government buyers
- Enable startup participation as subcontractors to established vendors
- Foster mentoring relationships between experienced and new entrants
- Build trust and relationships through repeated interactions

*Rationale:* Networks and partnerships help startups overcome information and capability gaps while managing risk.

#### 7.2.4 Institutional Change

**8. Reform Incentives and Accountability**

- Recognize and reward procurement officials who successfully source innovative solutions
- Create safe spaces for experimentation (innovation labs, pilot programs)
- Shift from compliance-focused to outcome-focused accountability
- Celebrate successes and learn from failures

*Rationale:* Institutional culture and incentives often constitute binding constraints on innovation procurement.

**9. Build Procurement Organization Capabilities**

- Train procurement officials in working with startups and evaluating innovation
- Develop understanding of digital technologies and startup ecosystems
- Create specialist roles (innovation procurement officers)
- Foster leadership commitment to innovation objectives

*Rationale:* Effective implementation requires capabilities that traditional procurement organizations often lack.

#### 7.2.5 Evaluation and Learning

**10. Implement Comprehensive Monitoring and Evaluation**

- Track startup participation, success rates, and outcomes over time
- Assess both individual startup outcomes and ecosystem effects
- Evaluate program effectiveness and cost-benefit
- Share learnings across jurisdictions and continuously improve

*Rationale:* Evidence-based policy improvement requires systematic evaluation beyond anecdotal success stories.

### 7.3 Implementation Considerations

Implementing these recommendations requires attention to:

**Sequencing:** Some reforms are prerequisites for others (e.g., digital platforms before marketplace programs)

**Resourcing:** Effective implementation requires investment in technology, personnel, and support services

**Stakeholder Engagement:** Success requires buy-in from procurement organizations, startups, established vendors, and political leadership

**Adaptation:** Generic recommendations must be tailored to specific jurisdictional contexts, existing frameworks, and local ecosystems

**Patience:** Ecosystem development effects emerge over years, not months; sustained commitment is essential

### 7.4 Research Agenda

This research opens several avenues for future investigation:

#### 7.4.1 Causal Identification

More rigorous causal analysis using:
- Natural experiments when policies are implemented at different times
- Randomized controlled trials of specific interventions
- Instrumental variables approaches
- Synthetic control methods

#### 7.4.2 Long-Term Outcomes

Longitudinal studies tracking:
- Startup survival and growth over 10+ years
- Career paths of startup founders
- Evolution of innovation ecosystems
- Sustainability of policy effects

#### 7.4.3 Comparative Expansion

Extending comparative analysis to:
- Additional jurisdictions and policy models
- Developing economy contexts
- Non-digital sectors
- Different government levels (local, state, federal)

#### 7.4.4 Mechanism Analysis

Deeper investigation of:
- How specific policy features affect outcomes
- Interactions among different policy instruments
- Role of institutional context and culture
- Network formation and ecosystem development processes

#### 7.4.5 Value and Impact Assessment

Comprehensive evaluation of:
- Public value created through innovation procurement
- Cost-benefit analysis of support programs
- Innovation and productivity spillovers
- Distributional effects and equity implications

### 7.5 Final Reflections

Public procurement represents a powerful but underutilized tool for supporting startup sustainability and fostering innovation. The research demonstrates that well-designed procurement policies can simultaneously advance multiple objectives: accessing innovative solutions, delivering public value, supporting economic development, and building vibrant innovation ecosystems.

However, realizing this potential requires more than marginal adjustments to existing systems. It demands:

- **Comprehensive Reform:** Addressing multiple barriers through coordinated policy bundles
- **Cultural Change:** Shifting procurement organizations toward innovation and risk-taking
- **Sustained Commitment:** Maintaining support over time horizons sufficient for ecosystem effects
- **Evidence-Based Practice:** Continuously evaluating, learning, and improving

The stakes are significant. Digital startups face substantial sustainability challenges, with many innovative ventures failing not due to product viability but market access difficulties. Simultaneously, governments face pressing challenges—from climate change to healthcare to digital transformation—that require innovative solutions.

Procurement policy sits at the intersection of these needs. By thoughtfully leveraging procurement to support startup sustainability, governments can access the innovative capabilities they need while strengthening the entrepreneurial ecosystems that drive economic dynamism and social progress.

The path forward is clear, though implementation will be challenging. The research provides both evidence and guidance for policymakers committed to harnessing procurement's potential. Success will require leadership, resources, persistence, and willingness to learn from both successes and failures.

Ultimately, leveraging procurement for startup sustainability is not about favoring one group of suppliers over others. It is about ensuring that procurement systems are open, accessible, and effective—enabling governments to access the best solutions regardless of supplier size or age while supporting the innovation ecosystems that benefit society as a whole.

### Appendix F: Glossary of Terms

**Digital Startup:** A company less than 5-7 years old that leverages digital technologies as core to its value proposition and has growth orientation.

**Public Procurement:** The process by which government entities acquire goods, services, and works from external suppliers.

**Value for Money:** Optimal balance of cost, quality, and outcomes in procurement.

**SME (Small and Medium Enterprise):** Business with employee count or revenue below specified thresholds (varies by jurisdiction).

**Set-Aside:** Procurement opportunity reserved for specific supplier categories (e.g., small businesses).

**SBIR (Small Business Innovation Research):** US program funding R&D by small businesses with commercialization potential.

**Framework Agreement:** Pre-negotiated terms enabling rapid contract awards within scope.

**Pre-Commercial Procurement:** Procurement of R&D services before solutions are commercially available.

**Innovation Procurement:** Procurement specifically designed to source innovative solutions.

**Valley of Death:** Period between initial funding and sustainable revenue where many startups fail.

**Bid Bond:** Financial guarantee submitted with bid to ensure contractor will execute contract if awarded.

**Performance Bond:** Guarantee that contractor will fulfill contract obligations.

**Payment Terms:** Agreed timeline for payment after invoice submission.

**Past Performance:** Prior contract execution history used in evaluation.

**Incumbent Advantage:** Structural advantages that established vendors have over new entrants.
